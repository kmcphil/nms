Keyword Guide — for Natalie

A working guide to what phrases to use where. Not a rulebook — a set of anchors.

Your website's voice is editorial and human. Editorial always wins over SEO. But if we weave the right phrases into the right sections, Google can actually find you when a founder searches for what you do.

Google (and now ChatGPT search, Perplexity, etc.) reads pages like a person: it looks at the headline first, the opening paragraph second, the subheads third, and the body copy last. Say what you do clearly at the top, and everything else follows.

1. Your keyword bank

Think of these as your approved vocabulary. They aren't slogans. You don't need to force them. But when you're deciding between two ways to say the same thing, pick the one from the list.

(Avoid "small business," "SMB," or "startup" — wrong audience for search intent.)

Location signal

Do NOT use these on your site

  • "Free strategy call" (kills lead quality)
  • "Full-service marketing"
  • "Marketing agency" (you're not one)
  • "Fractional CMO" (except to explicitly say you're not this)
  • "Coach" or "coaching"
  • "Boutique agency" for yourself now (it's your past — you're a consultancy now)
  • "Small business," "SMB," "solopreneur"
  • Generic marketing words: "engagement," "activation," "amplification" when they don't mean anything
  • Emojis in body copy

2. The rules of the road

  1. Say what you do in the first 20 words of any page. Not clever. Direct.
  2. Use one branded term + one category term per opening paragraph. Example: "I help founder-led businesses find their white space and clarify the story before more spend on execution." — "founder-led businesses" (audience) + "white space" (branded) + "clarify the story" (owned).
  3. Headlines should sound like Natalie. Subheads should sound like search. People read headlines for voice and subheads to skim. So headlines can be poetic ("Not a capability problem. A clarity problem."), but the subheads underneath should be plain ("Brand strategy for founder-led teams at an inflection point."). Give Google the plain version somewhere near the poetic one.
  4. The credentials matter for search. Tory Burch 2023 Fellow, former TV journalist, built and sold a boutique agency — these belong on Home, About, and once in the footer. They're not bragging; they're how Google (and prospects) verify who you are.
  5. Link between your pages using real words. When you say "the Sprint" in body copy, make it a link to the Sprint page. When you say "the framework," link to the Method section. Don't say "click here" or "learn more."
  6. Never write a paragraph for Google. If a sentence exists only to fit a keyword, delete it. Google is smart enough to notice, and readers definitely are.

RESULTS (/results)

The one thing this page must do for search: Build trust — but only with real proof.

START THE CONVERSATION (/start-the-conversation)

Not a page Google needs to optimize hard. But two rules:

  1. First sentence names what this is: "Tell me about your business and the strategic problem. I read every inquiry personally."
  2. Keep the "I read every inquiry" line. It's a phrase people quote when they refer you — helps branded search intent.

4. The one-page cheat sheet (print this)

Every page: - First 20 words say what you do and who you serve. - Full name spelled once on Home + About. - Location signal (Omaha) once on Home + About + Contact + footer. - Framework named exactly once as The White Space Growth Framework.

Every headline: - If it's poetic, put a plain-language subhead directly under it.

Every link inside body copy: - Use the real name of the destination as the link text. Never "click here."

Every service page: - Name the offer in the H1. - Say the category phrase in the first paragraph (positioning sprint / strategic advisor / brand repositioning). - Say what it isn't somewhere on the page. - Publish a starting price if approved.

Never: - Force a keyword into a sentence that doesn't want it. - Write for Google at the expense of voice. - Use words you wouldn't say out loud to a founder.

If a sentence passes the "would I actually say this to a founder on a fit call?" test, ship it. If not, cut it — whether it's SEO or not.
